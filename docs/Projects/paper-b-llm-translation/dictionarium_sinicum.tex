\begin{abstract}
We present \textit{Dictionarium Sinicum}, an open multilingual Chinese dictionary comprising 428,073 headwords with definitions in 12 languages, constructed by merging seven community-maintained dictionaries with glosses generated by a single LLM (MiniMax M2.5) at a total inference cost of {\raise.17ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}\$100. A three-phase pipeline---batch translation, retry, and context-aware backfill---achieves 100\% coverage across all 5.1M headword--language slots. We describe prompt engineering techniques that prevent script contamination in CJK-target languages, including Japanese kanji echo and Korean hanja mixing. Automated comparison against four community reference dictionaries over 453 headword--language pairs shows 87.3\% sense coverage with a 12.5\% false sense rate. The dictionary, augmented with 184K Cantonese and Hokkien dialect forms, is released under CC~BY-SA~4.0.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Bilingual and multilingual dictionaries remain essential infrastructure for language learning, translation, and NLP\@. For Chinese, the open-source community has produced high-quality dictionaries for a handful of language pairs---CC-CEDICT for English ({\raise.17ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}124K entries), HanDeDict for German ({\raise.17ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}159K entries), CFDICT for French ({\raise.17ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}56K entries), and CC-CIDICT for Indonesian ({\raise.17ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}124K entries)---but no comparable resources exist for Spanish, Korean, Russian, Vietnamese, Tagalog, Persian, or Swedish. Creating such dictionaries manually requires years of expert effort per language pair.

Recent advances in large language models suggest an alternative: using a single multilingual model to generate dictionary-quality definitions at scale. However, prior work on LLM-assisted lexicography has been limited to small-scale experiments. The closest precedent is a Pre-Qin philosophy lexicon project using Qwen3-14B to generate bilingual dictionary entries for approximately 1,000 classical Chinese philosophical terms in a single language pair \citep{preqin2026lexicon}. \citet{lu2024chainofdict} use existing bilingual dictionaries as chain-of-thought context to improve LLM translation of rare words---a complementary approach where dictionaries help LLMs translate, whereas we use LLMs to \textit{build} dictionaries. No published work has attempted dictionary generation at the scale of hundreds of thousands of headwords across more than two target languages.

We address this gap with \textit{Dictionarium Sinicum}, a system that:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Merges} seven community-maintained Chinese dictionaries covering 6 language pairs into a unified headword table of 428,073 entries.
\item \textbf{Translates} all headwords into 12 languages using a single LLM with carefully engineered prompts that produce dictionary-style output and prevent script contamination.
\item \textbf{Backfills} gaps using a context-aware prompt that sends existing translations as read-only context, ensuring consistency across languages.
\item \textbf{Augments} the dictionary with 184,000 dialect forms covering Cantonese (Jyutping) and Hokkien (POJ) pronunciations and lexical equivalents.
\end{enumerate}

The total inference cost was approximately \$100, and the full pipeline completed in under one week. The project's name honors the \textit{Dictionario Hispanico Sinicum} (1626--1642), a 27,000-entry Spanish-Chinese-Hokkien dictionary compiled by Dominican missionaries in Manila---a reminder that multilingual Chinese lexicography has a four-century history, and that the Hokkien maritime vocabulary in our dialect data traces the same trade routes documented in that manuscript.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Multilingual Lexical Resources}

Large-scale multilingual lexical resources have been constructed through both manual and automated methods. PanLex \citep{kamholz2014panlex} aggregates bilingual dictionaries into a graph covering {\raise.17ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}5,700 languages with over 1.2 billion pairwise translations, but its Chinese coverage is limited to headword-level mappings without definition glosses. BabelNet \citep{navigli2012babelnet} combines WordNet, Wikipedia, and other resources into a multilingual encyclopedic dictionary, but is not open-access for bulk redistribution and focuses on synset alignment rather than dictionary-style glosses. Our work differs from both in producing complete, human-readable definition glosses for a single source language across 12 targets, released under CC~BY-SA~4.0.

\subsection{LLM-Assisted Lexicography}

The use of LLMs for dictionary construction is an emerging area with limited published work. The closest precedent is a Pre-Qin philosophy lexicon \citep{preqin2026lexicon} that uses Qwen3-14B to generate {\raise.17ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}1,000 bilingual entries for classical Chinese philosophical terms---two orders of magnitude smaller than our work. Chain-of-Dictionary prompting \citep{lu2024chainofdict} uses existing bilingual dictionaries as chain-of-thought context to improve LLM translation of rare words---a complementary approach where dictionaries help LLMs translate, whereas we use LLMs to \textit{build} dictionaries.

\subsection{Multilingual Translation Models}

General-purpose multilingual translation models such as NLLB \citep{costajussa2022nllb} cover 200+ languages but are designed for sentence-level translation, not dictionary-style glosses. Tower \citep{alves2024tower} focuses on high-resource language pairs. Neither has been evaluated for or applied to dictionary construction. These models do not natively produce multi-sense gloss lists, making direct comparison nontrivial (see Limitations).

\subsection{Community Chinese Dictionaries}

The open Chinese dictionary ecosystem is built on the CC-CEDICT model: community-maintained, CEDICT-format text files released under Creative Commons licenses. CC-CEDICT \citep{cedict}, HanDeDict \citep{handedict}, CFDICT \citep{cfdict}, and CC-CIDICT \citep{cidict} together cover four European languages and Indonesian. Wiktextract \citep{wiktextract} provides multilingual glosses extracted from Wiktionary, and JMdict \citep{jmdict} covers Japanese. No comparable community dictionaries exist for Spanish, Korean, Russian, Vietnamese, Tagalog, Persian, or Swedish.

\section{Translation Pipeline}
\label{sec:pipeline}

\subsection{Model Selection}

We evaluated 20+ LLMs across 10 inference providers on a 5-headword test suite covering emergency service numbers (culturally specific), anime titles (transliteration challenges), and medical terminology. Selection criteria were: (1) correct 12-language output in the prescribed format, (2) no script contamination, (3) geographic context preservation, (4) sustainable throughput at 428K headwords, and (5) cost under \$200.

\begin{table}[t]
\centering

\begin{tabular}{llrl}

\midrule
MiniMax M2.5 & MiniMax & 1.2 & None \\
Kimi K2      & Groq & 1.8 & None \\
Nemotron 253B & NIM & 7.0 & None \\
Sonnet 4.6   & Anthropic & 19.8 & HW echo \\

MiniMax M2.1 & NIM & 167 & Think ON \\

\bottomrule

\caption{Model evaluation summary (5-headword test suite). HW echo = headword echo in output. Think ON = thinking mode silently enabled.}
\label{tab:models}
\end{table}

MiniMax M2.5 was selected for production: zero issues on the test suite, 1.2s/entry throughput, no observed rate limits with 20 parallel workers, and an estimated cost of \$93 for the full run (Table~\ref{tab:models}).

A notable finding is that the same open-weight model can produce dramatically different output depending on the inference provider. MiniMax M2.1 served by NVIDIA NIM took 167s per entry with thinking mode silently enabled, while the same weights on Fireworks took 5.4s with thinking disabled. Benchmark scores (SWE-bench, MMLU) showed no predictive value for this structured multilingual task. These observations align with emerging evidence that inference acceleration introduces unpredictable behavioral changes \citep{kirsten2025acceleration}.

\subsection{Prompt Engineering}

The production prompt generates all 12 languages simultaneously for each batch of 20 headwords. Key design decisions:

\paragraph{System prompt} (176 tokens): Establishes the lexicographer persona, output format (\texttt{xx: def1/def2}), conciseness constraints (max 5 glosses), and language-specific rules addressing three classes of script contamination:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Japanese kanji echo}: Without explicit instruction, LLMs frequently ``define'' a Chinese word in Japanese by repeating the same characters (e.g., 銀行 → 銀行). The prompt requires meaning explanations, not kanji echo.
\item \textbf{Korean hanja mixing}: Korean definitions often include Chinese characters (hanja) unrecognizable to most modern Korean readers. The prompt specifies Hangul only.
\item \textbf{General CJK leakage}: A blanket prohibition on Chinese characters in non-Chinese definitions.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{User prompt} (batch of 20): Each entry includes traditional/simplified characters, pinyin, POS tag, and all existing community definitions as context. This constitutes \textbf{pivot translation}: for the six languages without community dictionaries (Spanish, Korean, Russian, Vietnamese, Tagalog, Persian, Swedish), the model effectively translates from the English and other community-language glosses rather than directly from Chinese.

\paragraph{Token economics}: {\raise.17ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}5,500 tokens per batch (500 system + 2,000 input + 3,000 output). Full run: 21,403 batches.

This design ensures consistency: the model sees existing glosses and produces output that matches in style and specificity.

\subsection{Parser Robustness}

Parsing structured LLM output at scale required handling several failure modes: multi-language line collapse (the model outputs multiple languages on one line), numbered vs.\ unnumbered responses, and overwrite protection via three-layer guards (query filter, parser filter, database existence check).

\section{Quality Assessment}
\label{sec:quality}

\subsection{CJK Leak Audit}

We sampled 10,000 definitions per non-Japanese language (110,000 total) and measured CJK character contamination. The overall leak rate across non-Japanese languages was \textbf{2.49\%} of sampled definitions (weighted). Korean showed the highest rate (0.79\%) due to hanja mixing, followed by Tagalog (0.42\%) and Vietnamese (0.38\%).

This metric conflates three distinct phenomena: (1)~\textbf{genuine errors}---Chinese text serving no function in the target language; (2)~\textbf{conventional script mixing}---Korean hanja usage is standard in Korean technical dictionaries; Vietnamese Hán-Việt vocabulary is a fundamental lexical layer, not contamination; (3)~\textbf{metalinguistic references}---cross-reference entries necessarily cite Chinese characters. A cleanup script targets category~1 errors; categories~2 and~3 require language-specific policies.

\subsection{Comparison with Community Dictionaries}

For the four languages with existing community dictionaries providing glosses (English, German, French, Indonesian), we compared LLM-generated definitions against human-curated references.\footnote{JMdict was excluded because it stores kana readings rather than Japanese-language glosses.} We sampled 200 headwords stratified by community source coverage as a frequency proxy and measured two metrics across 453 headword--language pairs:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Sense coverage}: proportion of reference senses captured by the LLM gloss (automated lexical overlap with 50\% word-overlap threshold)
\item \textbf{False sense rate}: proportion of LLM glosses not matched by any reference sense
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Important caveat.} The translation prompt provides all existing community definitions as input context (\S\ref{sec:pipeline}). For these four languages, the model \textit{sees} the reference glosses and is asked to produce output in the same language. This comparison therefore measures \textbf{context utilization}---how faithfully the model preserves reference senses---not independent translation quality. We find that 42\% of English, 40\% of German, 55\% of French, and 60\% of Indonesian LLM glosses are substring matches of the corresponding community definition.

The overall sense coverage of 87.3\% (95\% bootstrap CI: [84.7\%, 89.8\%]) with a false sense rate of 12.5\% (95\% CI: [10.1\%, 15.0\%]) shows that the model successfully preserves most reference senses while introducing few unsupported glosses (Tables~\ref{tab:comparison}--\ref{tab:bands}). Manual inspection reveals that most ``false senses'' are legitimate synonyms absent from the reference.

The main failure mode is \textbf{sense compression}: the LLM produces fewer glosses than community dictionaries (median 2 vs.\ 3), preferring the most common translation equivalent and omitting specialized or archaic senses. This raises a linguistically interesting question: does the model compress senses uniformly, or does it systematically drop certain sense types (archaic, domain-specific, colloquial)? Preliminary inspection suggests the latter, but a controlled study with sense-type annotation is needed.

For the six languages \textit{without} community dictionaries, the model relies on pivot translation and we have no external reference for quality assessment.

\subsection{Source Priority and Definition Merging}

For headwords with definitions from multiple sources, we apply a priority hierarchy: community dictionaries > Wiktextract > MiniMax. Human-curated definitions take precedence, with LLM translations filling gaps for languages without community dictionaries.

\subsection{Coverage Summary}

Every headword--language slot (428,073 $\times$ 12 = 5,136,876) has an LLM-generated gloss. For the 739,305 slots also covered by a community dictionary, both definitions are stored; the downstream application applies source priority to select which definition the end user sees.

\section{Downstream Application}
\label{sec:downstream}

To validate the dictionary's practical utility, we built a language pack generator that produces language-specific SQLite databases for two Chinese learning applications. The generator swaps in definitions from the target language, rebuilds search indexes, and generates cross-reference terms. Output sizes range from 61--67~MB (30,546 single-character entries) to 327--384~MB (all 428,073 entries) per language, with build times of 5--140 seconds. The dictionary is currently deployed in production for English and Spanish.

\section*{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\textbf{Glosses, not definitions.} The resource produces translation equivalents (glosses), not full lexicographic definitions with usage notes, example sentences, grammatical patterns, or register markers. Entries lack sense discrimination: a polysemous word like 打 produces a flat list without indicating which target-language verb corresponds to which Chinese sense. For inflecting target languages, bare citation forms lack grammatical metadata (German noun gender, Russian aspect pairs).

\textbf{Evaluation limitations.} The 87.3\% sense coverage figure is an automated lexical overlap proxy, not a human judgment, and the evaluation is partially circular (\S\ref{sec:quality}). For the six languages without community references, we have no external quality measure. Comparison against MT baselines (NLLB, Google Translate) remains future work.

\textbf{Pivot translation bias.} All translations are mediated through community-language context (primarily English), inheriting English sense boundaries. The LLM may partially bypass this via its multilingual training, but direct Chinese$\rightarrow$target evaluation is needed.

\textbf{Single-model homogeneity.} All 5.1M LLM-generated glosses originate from a single model on a single API\@. Model-specific biases propagate uniformly, and reproducibility depends on this API remaining available.

\textbf{Headword and language selection.} The 428K headwords are inherited from CC-CEDICT without curation; the inventory includes phone numbers, anime titles, and proper nouns. The 12 target languages overrepresent European languages (5 of 12) and exclude Hindi, Arabic, Thai, and Malay.

\textbf{No interchange format.} The resource is stored as flat text in SQLite without compliance with TEI-Lex-0, OntoLex-Lemon, or ISO~24613~LMF, limiting interoperability.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future}

\textbf{From glossary to dictionary.} Sense-discriminated entries mapping each target-language gloss to a specific Chinese sense with usage guidance. LLMs could produce numbered sense-aligned translations, or multilingual embeddings could perform post-hoc alignment.

\textbf{Pivot translation bias as a window into LLM representations.} A controlled experiment comparing pivot-translated glosses against direct translations across typologically diverse languages would test whether LLMs reproduce English sense boundaries or restructure senses from their multilingual training.

\textbf{Computational loanword detection across the Sinosphere.} The dictionary now contains both Hokkien source forms and Southeast Asian target languages---infrastructure for tracing lexical diffusion at scale. Linking Hokkien \textit{tāu-hū} to Indonesian \textit{tahu}, Tagalog \textit{taho}, and Thai \foreignlanguage{thai}{เต้าหู้} would produce a machine-readable atlas of Chinese vocabulary diffusion across maritime Southeast Asia \citep{chanyap1980}.

\textbf{Under-resourced and endangered languages.} The {\raise.17ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}\$100 cost makes the pipeline relevant for languages without institutional support for manual lexicography. Within China alone, Zhuang (16M speakers), Tibetan (6M), Uyghur (10M), and Yi (7.8M) lack comprehensive bilingual dictionaries.

\textbf{Community-in-the-loop evaluation.} Native-speaker evaluation across all 12 target languages---particularly by diaspora communities who are primary users of bilingual dictionaries---would establish ground-truth quality metrics.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We have demonstrated that a single commercial LLM can produce a multilingual Chinese glossary of unprecedented scale---428,073 headwords in 12 languages---for approximately \$100 in API inference costs. The key enablers are (1)~batched prompting that generates all languages simultaneously, (2)~language-specific prompt rules that prevent script contamination, and (3)~a context-aware backfill pipeline that ensures complete coverage. The resulting resource, combined with community dictionaries and 184K dialect forms spanning Cantonese and Hokkien, provides the first open multilingual Chinese lexical resource covering major languages of Europe, East Asia, Southeast Asia, and the Middle East.

The resource is a beginning, not an endpoint. Transforming gloss lists into sense-structured, grammatically enriched, human-validated dictionary entries---and extending the approach to under-resourced languages---is a multiyear research program requiring expertise in computational lexicography, multilingual NLP, lexical typology, and community-based language documentation. The scientific questions this work opens---How do LLMs represent cross-lingual lexical semantics? Does pivot translation reproduce or restructure sense boundaries? Can computational methods trace historical loanword diffusion across language families?---are as valuable as the artifact itself. We release the complete resource, build tools, and evaluation data under CC~BY-SA~4.0, and invite the community to build upon it.
