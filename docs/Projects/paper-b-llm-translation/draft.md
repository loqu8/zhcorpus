# Dictionarium Sinicum: Building a 428K-Headword Chinese Dictionary in 12 Languages with a Single LLM for Under $100

## Abstract

We present *Dictionarium Sinicum*, an open multilingual Chinese dictionary comprising 428,073 headwords with definitions in 12 languages, constructed by merging seven community-maintained dictionaries with glosses generated by a single LLM (MiniMax M2.5) at a total inference cost of ~$100. A three-phase pipeline — batch translation, retry, and context-aware backfill — achieves 100% coverage across all 5.1M headword-language slots. We describe prompt engineering techniques that prevent script contamination in CJK-target languages, including Japanese kanji echo and Korean hanja mixing. Automated comparison against four community reference dictionaries over 453 headword-language pairs shows 87.3% sense coverage with a 12.5% false sense rate. The dictionary, augmented with 184K Cantonese and Hokkien dialect forms, is released under CC BY-SA 4.0.

## 1. Introduction

Bilingual and multilingual dictionaries remain essential infrastructure for language learning, translation, and NLP. For Chinese, the open-source community has produced high-quality dictionaries for a handful of language pairs — CC-CEDICT for English (~124K entries), HanDeDict for German (~159K entries), CFDICT for French (~56K entries), and CC-CIDICT for Indonesian (~124K entries) — but no comparable resources exist for Spanish, Korean, Russian, Vietnamese, Tagalog, Persian, or Swedish. Creating such dictionaries manually requires years of expert effort per language pair.

Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) suggest an alternative: using a single multilingual model to generate dictionary-quality definitions at scale. However, prior work on LLM-assisted lexicography has been limited to small-scale experiments. The closest precedent is a Pre-Qin philosophy lexicon project using Qwen3-14B to generate bilingual dictionary entries for approximately 1,000 classical Chinese philosophical terms in a single language pair (npj Heritage Science, 2026). Lu et al. (EMNLP 2024) use existing bilingual dictionaries as chain-of-thought context to improve LLM translation, but this work uses dictionaries to help LLMs translate text, not to generate new dictionary entries. No published work has attempted dictionary generation at the scale of hundreds of thousands of headwords across more than two target languages.

We address this gap with *Dictionarium Sinicum*, a system that:

1. **Merges** seven community-maintained Chinese dictionaries (CC-CEDICT, HanDeDict, CFDICT, CC-CIDICT, CHEDICC, Wiktextract, JMdict) covering 6 language pairs into a unified headword table of 428,073 entries.
2. **Translates** all headwords into 12 languages using a single LLM (MiniMax M2.5) with carefully engineered prompts that produce dictionary-style output and prevent script contamination.
3. **Backfills** gaps using a context-aware prompt that sends existing translations as read-only context, ensuring consistency across languages.
4. **Augments** the dictionary with 184,000 dialect forms covering Cantonese (Jyutping) and Hokkien (POJ) pronunciations and lexical equivalents.

The total inference cost was approximately $100, and the full pipeline was completed in under one week. The project's name honors the *Dictionario Hispanico Sinicum* (1626-1642), a 27,000-entry Spanish-Chinese-Hokkien dictionary compiled by Dominican missionaries in Manila — a reminder that multilingual Chinese lexicography has a four-century history, and that the Hokkien maritime vocabulary in our dialect data traces the same trade routes documented in that manuscript.

## 2. Related Work

### 2.1 Multilingual Lexical Resources

Large-scale multilingual lexical resources have been constructed through both manual and automated methods. PanLex (Kamholz et al., 2014) aggregates bilingual dictionaries into a graph covering ~5,700 languages with over 1.2 billion pairwise translations, but its Chinese coverage is limited to headword-level mappings without definition glosses. BabelNet (Navigli and Ponzetto, 2012) combines WordNet, Wikipedia, and other resources into a multilingual encyclopedic dictionary, but is not open-access for bulk redistribution and focuses on synset alignment rather than dictionary-style glosses. Our work differs from both in producing complete, human-readable definition glosses (not just translation equivalents or synset links) for a single source language across 12 targets, released under CC BY-SA 4.0.

### 2.2 LLM-Assisted Lexicography

The use of LLMs for dictionary construction is an emerging area with limited published work. The closest precedent is a Pre-Qin philosophy lexicon (npj Heritage Science, 2026) that uses Qwen3-14B to generate ~1,000 bilingual entries for classical Chinese philosophical terms — two orders of magnitude smaller than our work. Chain-of-Dictionary prompting (Lu et al., EMNLP 2024) uses existing bilingual dictionaries as chain-of-thought context to improve LLM translation of rare words — a complementary approach where dictionaries help LLMs translate, whereas we use LLMs to *build* dictionaries.

### 2.3 Multilingual Translation Models

General-purpose multilingual translation models such as NLLB (Costa-jussà et al., 2022) cover 200+ languages but are designed for sentence-level translation, not dictionary-style glosses. Tower (Alves et al., 2024) focuses on high-resource language pairs. Neither has been evaluated for or applied to dictionary construction. These models are designed for sentence-level translation and do not natively produce multi-sense gloss lists, making direct comparison nontrivial (see Limitations).

### 2.4 Community Chinese Dictionaries

The open Chinese dictionary ecosystem is built on the CC-CEDICT model: community-maintained, CEDICT-format text files released under Creative Commons licenses. CC-CEDICT (English, CC BY-SA 4.0, ~124K entries), HanDeDict (German, CC BY-SA 3.0, ~159K entries), CFDICT (French, CC BY-SA 3.0, ~56K entries), and CC-CIDICT (Indonesian, CC BY-SA 4.0, ~124K entries) together cover four European languages and Indonesian. Wiktextract provides multilingual glosses extracted from Wiktionary, and JMdict covers Japanese. No comparable community dictionaries exist for Spanish, Korean, Russian, Vietnamese, Tagalog, Persian, or Swedish.

### 3.2 Headword Unification

Source dictionaries use varying conventions for pinyin romanization, traditional/simplified character pairs, and entry granularity. We unified headwords on the triple (traditional, simplified, pinyin), yielding 428,073 unique headwords. Of these, 30,546 are single-character entries covering 19,471 distinct Chinese characters (many characters have multiple pinyin readings — e.g., 参 has 12 distinct entries).

Hokkien romanization uses two systems: iTaigi provides Tâi-lô (臺灣閩南語羅馬字拼音方案, the MOE standard), while TaiHua uses traditional POJ (Pe̍h-ōe-jī). The two systems differ in tone marking conventions but are mutually intelligible; we store each in its source format and note the system in the `source` field. A distinctive feature of Hokkien is its high lexical divergence from Mandarin: 68-78% of Hokkien forms use different characters entirely (e.g., 漂亮 *piàoliang* "beautiful" → 媠 *suí*; 東西 *dōngxi* "thing" → 物件 *mi̍h-kiānn*).

## 4. Translation Pipeline

### 4.1 Model Selection

We evaluated 20+ LLMs across 10 inference providers (Table 2) on a 5-headword test suite covering emergency service numbers (culturally specific), anime titles (transliteration challenges), and medical terminology. Selection criteria were:

1. **Correct 12-language output** in the prescribed `xx: def1/def2` format
2. **No script contamination** (Chinese characters leaking into non-CJK definitions)
3. **Geographic context preservation** (e.g., "Mainland China and Taiwan" for 110)
4. **Sustainable throughput** at the scale of 428K headwords
5. **Cost** under $200 for the complete run

**Table 2: Model Evaluation Summary (5-headword test suite)**

| Model | Provider | Time/entry | Output Issues | Est. Cost |
|-------|----------|-----------|---------------|-----------|
| MiniMax M2.5 | MiniMax API | 1.2s | None | **$93** |
| Kimi K2 Instruct | Groq | 1.8s | None | $246 |
| Nemotron Ultra 253B | NVIDIA NIM | 7.0s | None | Credits only |
| Claude Sonnet 4.6 | Anthropic | 19.8s | Headword echo | $1,124 |
| DeepSeek V3.2 | NVIDIA NIM | 35.3s | Headword echo | N/A |
| MiniMax M2.1 | NVIDIA NIM | 167s | Thinking mode forced ON | N/A |
| MiniMax M2.1 | Fireworks | 5.4s | None | $93 |

MiniMax M2.5 was selected for production: zero issues on the test suite, 1.2s/entry throughput, no observed rate limits with 20 parallel workers, and an estimated cost of $93 for the full run.

A notable finding from this evaluation is that the same open-weight model can produce dramatically different output depending on the inference provider. MiniMax M2.1 served by NVIDIA NIM took 167s per entry with thinking mode silently enabled, while the same weights on Fireworks took 5.4s with thinking disabled. Llama 4 Maverick echoed raw headwords on NIM but produced clean output on SambaNova, suggesting different quantization or prompt preprocessing. Benchmark scores (SWE-bench, MMLU) showed no predictive value for this structured multilingual task: DeepSeek V3.2 (SWE-bench 73.1%) echoed headwords, while MiniMax M2.5 (unranked) produced flawless output. A systematic study of these cross-provider divergences is beyond the scope of this paper, but our observations align with emerging evidence that inference acceleration introduces unpredictable behavioral changes (Kirsten et al., NAACL 2025).

### 4.2 Prompt Engineering

The production prompt generates all 12 languages simultaneously for each batch of 20 headwords. Key design decisions:

**System prompt** (176 tokens): Establishes the lexicographer persona, output format (`xx: def1/def2`), conciseness constraints (max 5 glosses), and language-specific rules addressing three classes of script contamination:

1. **Japanese kanji echo**: Without explicit instruction, LLMs frequently "define" a Chinese word in Japanese by simply repeating the same characters (e.g., 銀行 → 銀行). The prompt requires: *"Provide the MEANING in Japanese, not just kanji echo or kana reading. Write a Japanese definition/gloss that explains the word."*

2. **Korean hanja mixing**: Korean definitions often include Chinese characters (hanja) that would be unrecognizable to most modern Korean readers. The prompt specifies: *"Write in Hangul only. Do NOT mix in Chinese characters (漢字/한자)."*

3. **General CJK leakage**: A blanket prohibition: *"Every non-Chinese definition must contain ZERO Chinese characters (汉字)."*

**User prompt** (batch of 20): Each entry includes traditional/simplified characters, pinyin, POS tag, and all existing community definitions as context. For headwords with CC-CEDICT, HanDeDict, CFDICT, or CC-CIDICT entries, the model sees those definitions to ground its output. This constitutes **pivot translation**: for the six languages without community dictionaries (Spanish, Korean, Russian, Vietnamese, Tagalog, Persian, Swedish), the model effectively translates from the English and other community-language glosses rather than directly from Chinese. We accept this trade-off because the alternative — generating all 12 languages without any context — produces lower-quality output with more script contamination and sense drift. The prompt concludes with explicit `xx:` placeholders for all 12 target languages.

**Token economics**: ~5,500 tokens per batch (500 system + 2,000 input + 3,000 output). Full run: 21,403 batches.

### 4.3 Three-Phase Execution

| Phase | Description | Entries | Definitions | Time | Rate | Cost |
|-------|-------------|---------|------------|------|------|------|
| 1. Main run | Full 12-language translation | 374,602 | 4,410,579 | ~24h | 4.6/s | ~$93 |
| 2. Retry | Zero-def headwords from phase 1 | 53,471 | 616,276 | 92min | 5.4/s | ~$3 |
| 3. Backfill | Partial-coverage headwords | 82,550+ | 110,345+ | ~105min | 8-14/s | ~$4 |
| **Total** | | **428,073** | **5,876,181** | ~27h | | **~$100** |

**Phase 1** processed all 428,073 headwords in batches of 20 with 20 parallel workers. The API returned unparseable responses for 53,471 headwords (12.5%), primarily due to batch-level formatting errors where the model's response was cut off or malformed.

**Phase 2** retried the failed headwords using the same prompt and pipeline, recovering 616,276 additional definitions and leaving 5,875 headwords with partial coverage (< 12 languages).

**Phase 3** used a distinct context-aware backfill prompt (`backfill_langs.py`) that sends all existing translations as read-only context, asking the model to produce *only* the missing languages. This design ensures consistency: the model sees "en: bank / de: Bank / fr: banque" and produces a Spanish definition that matches in style and specificity. Three backfill passes on 2026-03-07 closed all remaining gaps, achieving 100.0% coverage across all 428,073 × 12 = 5,136,876 language slots.

### 4.4 Parser Robustness

Parsing structured LLM output at scale required handling several failure modes:

- **Multi-language line collapse**: The model sometimes outputs `en: bank/de: Bank/fr: banque` on a single line. A regex-based normalizer splits on `/xx:` boundaries where `xx` is a known language code.
- **Numbered vs unnumbered responses**: The backfill prompt, which often requests a single language per entry, causes the model to drop entry numbers. The parser detects this and uses blank lines as entry separators.
- **Overwrite protection**: Three layers prevent backfill from corrupting existing data: (1) query filter returns only headwords with < 12 languages, (2) parser only accepts languages listed in the missing set, (3) database existence check before every write.

## 5. Quality Assessment

### 5.1 CJK Leak Audit

We sampled 10,000 definitions per non-Japanese language (110,000 total) and measured CJK character contamination:

| Language | Leaks / 10K | Rate | Primary Cause |
|----------|------------|------|---------------|
| Korean | 79 | 0.79% | Hanja mixing (加工하다, 薄く하다) |
| Tagalog | 42 | 0.42% | Cross-reference entries |
| Vietnamese | 38 | 0.38% | Sino-Vietnamese terms |
| Russian | 27 | 0.27% | Cross-reference entries |
| Persian | 25 | 0.25% | Cross-reference entries |
| German | 18 | 0.18% | Compound words with CJK |
| French | 15 | 0.15% | Cross-reference entries |
| English | 12 | 0.12% | Cross-reference entries |
| Spanish | 10 | 0.10% | Cross-reference entries |
| Indonesian | 8 | 0.08% | Rare |
| Swedish | 6 | 0.06% | Rare |

Overall CJK leak rate across non-Japanese languages: **2.49% of sampled definitions** (weighted). We note that this metric conflates three distinct phenomena:

1. **Genuine errors**: Chinese text that serves no function in the target language (e.g., a German definition containing 银行 instead of "Bank").
2. **Conventional script mixing**: Korean hanja usage (加工하다) is standard in Korean technical dictionaries such as 국어대사전. Vietnamese Hán-Việt (漢越) vocabulary is a fundamental lexical layer, not contamination. Our zero-tolerance prompt rule overrejects legitimate target-language conventions.
3. **Metalinguistic references**: Cross-reference entries (e.g., "variant of 落 [luo4]") necessarily cite Chinese characters.

The dominant category varies by language: Korean "leaks" are primarily category 2 (conventional hanja), while English/French/German leaks are primarily category 3 (cross-references). A cleanup script (`fix_cjk_leaks.py`) targets category 1 errors; categories 2 and 3 require language-specific policies that we have not yet implemented.

### 5.2 Comparison with Community Dictionaries

For the four languages with existing community dictionaries that provide glosses (English, German, French, Indonesian), we compared LLM-generated definitions against human-curated references. JMdict was excluded because it stores kana readings rather than Japanese-language glosses, making gloss-level comparison impossible. We sampled 200 headwords stratified by community source coverage as a frequency proxy (high: defined in 4+ sources, mid: 3, low: 2, rare: 1) and measured two metrics across 453 headword-language pairs:

1. **Sense coverage**: proportion of reference senses captured by the LLM gloss (automated lexical overlap with 50% word-overlap threshold)
2. **False sense rate**: proportion of LLM glosses not matched by any reference sense

**Important caveat**: The translation prompt provides all existing community definitions as input context (Section 4.2). For these four languages, the model *sees* the reference glosses and is asked to produce output in the same language. This comparison therefore measures **context utilization** — how faithfully the model preserves reference senses — not independent translation quality. We find that 42% of English, 40% of German, 55% of French, and 60% of Indonesian LLM glosses are substring matches of the corresponding community definition. This is by design: for languages with community dictionaries, we *want* the model to preserve established glosses rather than inventing alternatives. The evaluation quantifies the residual divergence.

**Table 3: LLM vs Community Dictionary Comparison by Language**

| Language | Reference | n | Sense Coverage | False Sense Rate |
|----------|-----------|---|---------------|-----------------|
| English | CC-CEDICT | 139 | 85.8% | 14.1% |
| German | HanDeDict | 105 | 87.9% | 17.9% |
| French | CFDICT | 70 | 81.2% | 18.6% |
| Indonesian | CC-CIDICT | 139 | 91.3% | 3.7% |
| **Overall** | | **453** | **87.3%** | **12.5%** |

**Table 4: Comparison by Frequency Band**

| Band | n | Sense Coverage | False Sense Rate |
|------|---|---------------|-----------------|
| high (4+ sources) | 190 | 84.7% | 16.7% |
| mid (3 sources) | 146 | 87.4% | 11.8% |
| low (2 sources) | 94 | 90.8% | 5.7% |
| rare (1 source) | 23 | 93.5% | 10.9% |

The overall sense coverage of 87.3% (95% bootstrap CI: [84.7%, 89.8%]) with a false sense rate of 12.5% (95% CI: [10.1%, 15.0%]) shows that the model successfully preserves most reference senses while introducing few unsupported glosses. Manual inspection reveals that most "false senses" are legitimate synonyms absent from the reference (e.g., "embankment" for 堤坝 is a valid translation not listed in CC-CEDICT).

The main failure mode is **sense compression**: the LLM produces fewer glosses than community dictionaries (median 2 vs 3), preferring the most common translation equivalent and omitting specialized or archaic senses. For example, 客栈 is glossed as just "inn" by MiniMax, while CC-CEDICT lists "tavern / guest house / inn / hotel." This pattern raises a linguistically interesting question: does the model compress senses uniformly, or does it systematically drop certain sense types (archaic, domain-specific, colloquial)? Preliminary inspection suggests the latter — archaic and register-marked senses are dropped preferentially — but a controlled study with sense-type annotation is needed to confirm this. Such a study would contribute to our understanding of how LLMs internalize and reproduce lexical polysemy structure.

For the six languages *without* community dictionaries (Spanish, Korean, Russian, Vietnamese, Tagalog, Persian, Swedish), the model relies on the English and other community-language context as a pivot. We have no external reference for these languages and cannot make quality claims about them.

### 5.3 Source Priority and Definition Merging

For headwords with definitions from multiple sources, we apply a priority hierarchy: community dictionaries (CC-CEDICT, HanDeDict, CFDICT, CC-CIDICT, JMdict) > Wiktextract > MiniMax. This ensures that human-curated definitions take precedence, with LLM translations filling gaps for languages without community dictionaries (Spanish, Korean, Russian, Vietnamese, Tagalog, Persian, Swedish).

## 6. Downstream Application: Language Pack Generation

To validate the dictionary's practical utility, we built a language pack generator that produces language-specific SQLite databases for two Chinese learning applications (Copyworks for character study, Intuition for full dictionary). The generator swaps in definitions from the target language, rebuilds search indexes, and generates cross-reference terms. Output sizes range from 61-67 MB (30,546 single-character entries) to 327-384 MB (all 428,073 entries) per language, with build times of 5-140 seconds per language. The dictionary is currently deployed in production for English and Spanish.

## 7. Limitations

**Glosses, not definitions.** The resource produces translation equivalents (glosses), not full lexicographic definitions with usage notes, example sentences, grammatical patterns, or register markers. Entries lack sense discrimination: a polysemous word like 打 produces a flat list ("golpear / jugar / hacer" in Spanish) without indicating which target-language verb corresponds to which Chinese sense. For inflecting target languages, bare citation forms lack grammatical metadata (German noun gender, Russian aspect pairs). The resource is best understood as a multilingual gloss list in the tradition of CC-CEDICT, not a professional dictionary in the sense of the *Oxford Chinese Dictionary*.

**Evaluation limitations.** The 87.3% sense coverage figure is an automated lexical overlap proxy, not a human judgment, and the evaluation is partially circular: the model sees community definitions as input context (Section 5.2). For the six languages without community references (Spanish, Korean, Russian, Vietnamese, Tagalog, Persian, Swedish), we have no external quality measure at all. Comparison against MT baselines (NLLB, Google Translate) remains future work.

**Pivot translation bias.** All translations are mediated through community-language context (primarily English), inheriting English sense boundaries. Where English conflates two Chinese meanings, all target languages will too. The LLM may partially bypass this via its multilingual training, but direct Chinese→target evaluation is needed to quantify the effect.

**Single-model homogeneity.** All 5.1M LLM-generated glosses originate from a single model (MiniMax M2.5) on a single commercial API. Model-specific biases in translation style, sense selection, and cultural framing propagate uniformly. The pipeline's reproducibility depends on this API remaining available.

**Headword and language selection.** The 428K headwords are inherited from CC-CEDICT without curation; the inventory includes phone numbers, anime titles, and proper nouns alongside common vocabulary. The 12 target languages overrepresent European languages (5 of 12) and exclude Hindi, Arabic, Thai, and Malay — languages with large Chinese-speaking diaspora communities.

**No interchange format.** The resource is stored as flat text in SQLite without compliance with TEI-Lex-0, OntoLex-Lemon, or ISO 24613 LMF, limiting interoperability with existing dictionary infrastructure.

## 8. Future Work

**From glossary to dictionary.** The most ambitious next step is sense-discriminated entries — mapping each target-language gloss to a specific Chinese sense with usage guidance. LLMs could be prompted to produce numbered sense-aligned translations, or multilingual embeddings could perform post-hoc alignment. Combined with grammatical enrichment (German noun gender, Russian aspect pairs) through targeted LLM passes, this would yield the first open, sense-structured multilingual Chinese dictionary.

**Pivot translation bias as a window into LLM representations.** A controlled experiment comparing pivot-translated glosses (Chinese→English→target) against direct translations (Chinese→target) across typologically diverse languages (agglutinative Korean, fusional Russian, isolating Vietnamese) would test whether LLMs reproduce English sense boundaries or restructure senses from their multilingual training. This question has implications beyond lexicography: it probes whether multilingual pretraining creates genuine interlingual representations or merely English-mediated mappings.

**Computational loanword detection across the Sinosphere.** The dictionary now contains both Hokkien source forms and Southeast Asian target languages in a single resource — infrastructure for tracing lexical diffusion at scale. Linking Hokkien *tāu-hū* to Indonesian *tahu*, Tagalog *taho*, and Thai เต้าหู้ would produce a machine-readable atlas of Chinese vocabulary diffusion across maritime Southeast Asia, connecting 163+ documented Hokkien-Tagalog borrowings (Chan-Yap, 1980) to their cognates in Indonesian and Vietnamese.

**Under-resourced and endangered languages.** The ~$100 cost makes the pipeline relevant for languages without institutional support for manual lexicography. Within China alone, Zhuang (16M speakers), Tibetan (6M), Uyghur (10M), and Yi (7.8M) lack comprehensive bilingual dictionaries. The critical research question is how pipeline quality degrades as seed dictionary coverage and LLM training data decrease — answerable empirically with a small number of pilot languages at minimal cost.

**Community-in-the-loop evaluation.** Native-speaker evaluation across all 12 target languages — particularly by diaspora communities who are primary users of bilingual dictionaries — would establish ground-truth quality metrics, address cultural appropriateness, and generate training data for automatic quality estimation applicable to future LLM-generated dictionaries in other language families.

## 9. Conclusion

We have demonstrated that a single commercial LLM can produce a multilingual Chinese glossary of unprecedented scale — 428,073 headwords in 12 languages — for approximately $100 in API inference costs, excluding developer time for prompt engineering, model evaluation, and pipeline development. The key enablers are (1) batched prompting that generates all languages simultaneously, (2) language-specific prompt rules that prevent script contamination, and (3) a context-aware backfill pipeline that ensures complete coverage. The resulting resource, combined with community dictionaries and 184K dialect forms spanning Cantonese and Hokkien, provides the first open multilingual Chinese lexical resource covering major languages of Europe, East Asia, Southeast Asia, and the Middle East.

The resource is a beginning, not an endpoint. Transforming gloss lists into sense-structured, grammatically enriched, human-validated dictionary entries — and extending the approach to under-resourced languages where the need is greatest — is a multiyear research program requiring expertise in computational lexicography, multilingual NLP, lexical typology, and community-based language documentation. The scientific questions this work opens — How do LLMs represent cross-lingual lexical semantics? Does pivot translation reproduce or restructure sense boundaries? Can computational methods trace historical loanword diffusion across language families? — are as valuable as the artifact itself. We release the complete resource, build tools, and evaluation data at [repository URL] under CC BY-SA 4.0, and invite the community to build upon it.
